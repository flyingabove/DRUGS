# Problem Definition

## In One Sentence

Find a way to selectively kill the small population of leukemic stem cells (LSCs) that survives
standard AML treatment and lingers in the body after a patient reaches remission — because these are
the cells that make the cancer come back.

## The Situation We're Solving For

1. A patient is diagnosed with AML and undergoes standard treatment (chemotherapy, possibly targeted
   drugs, possibly a stem cell transplant).
2. Treatment works — the patient reaches **remission**, meaning doctors can no longer detect
   leukemia using standard tests.
3. Despite that, a tiny number of LSCs often survive treatment. They're not visible on standard
   remission testing (that's part of why they're dangerous), but can be detected as **measurable
   residual disease (MRD)** using more sensitive tests.
4. These surviving LSCs are the "pecky," lingering ones — largely dormant, already proven resistant
   to the first round of treatment (they're the ones that didn't die). Left alone, they eventually
   regrow into full relapse, which is the leading cause of AML treatment failure and death.

## What We're NOT Solving For

- Not a first-line treatment for newly diagnosed AML (existing treatment already gets most patients
  into remission).
- Not shrinking or killing the bulk tumor.
- Not preventing AML from occurring in the first place.
- Scope is specifically: the small, resistant LSC population left behind *after* successful initial
  treatment, that can reverse a patient's remission.

## Why This Is Hard

- **They look like normal cells.** LSCs closely resemble the patient's own healthy blood stem cells
  (HSCs). A drug that can't tell them apart wipes out the patient's normal blood-making system along
  with the cancer.
- **They're already the resistant survivors.** These specific LSCs already survived one round of
  aggressive chemo — by definition, they're the most treatment-resistant subset that exists.
- **They're dormant.** Most chemo drugs target actively dividing cells. Quiescent (non-dividing)
  LSCs are effectively invisible to that kind of attack, which is part of how they survive in the
  first place.

## What Success Looks Like

A therapeutic (or combination of therapeutics) that, given to a patient after they reach remission,
eliminates or durably suppresses residual LSCs — measured by reduced/undetectable MRD and, ultimately,
fewer or delayed relapses — without meaningfully damaging the patient's normal blood-forming stem
cells.
